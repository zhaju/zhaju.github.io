### Proving adequacy and inadequacy of a set of connectives
> Def: Adequacy
> A set S of connectives is called adequate iff any n-ary connective can be defined in terms of the connectives in S. For instance, the set $S_0 = \{\neg, \land , \lor\}$ is adequate
- **Proving adequacy:** Show that all connectives in an adequate set $S_0=\{\neg, \land, \lor\}$ can be defined in terms of the connectives in $S$

## 1.6 | Formal deduction in propositional logic
### Formal deducibility in propositional logic
> Formal Deducibility
> $\vdash$ has a similar meaning to $\models$, signifying argument validity
> To write that $A$ is formally deducible (or provable) from $\Sigma$, we write
$$
\Sigma\vdash A
$$

11 rules of formal deduction (but I'm only writing the 3 coolest ones)
$$
(\neg, -)
$$
If $\Sigma, \neg A\vdash B$, and $\Sigma, \neg A\vdash\neg B$, then $\Sigma\vdash A$ (proof by contradiction)
$$
(\lor, -)
$$
If $\Sigma, A\vdash C$, and $\Sigma, B\vdash C$, then $\Sigma, A\lor B\vdash C$ (proof by cases)
$$
(\implies, +)
$$
If $\Sigma, A\vdash B$, then $\Sigma\models A\implies B$ (assumption of a premise is the antecedent of a conditional)
### Definition of "formal deducibility" $(\vdash)$
> Definition: Formal deducibility
> A formula $A$ is formally deducible from $\Sigma$ ($\Sigma\vdash A$) if A is generated by a finite number of applications of the 11 rules of formal deduction
- Subsequent steps are **generated by a rule of formal deduction**
- The sequence of steps is called a **formal proof** of the last term $\Sigma_n\vdash A_n$

Checking a proof: Check each step
Finding a proof: Work in reverse

Semantics vs Syntax:
- Tautological consequence $(\Sigma\models A)$ is semantics
- Deducibility $(\Sigma\vdash A)$ is syntax

Proving statements about formal induction: Use structural induction on the **set of theorems**
### Theorems: Finiteness of premise set, reduction ad absurdum, transitivity
> Theorem: Finiteness of premise set
> If $\Sigma\vdash A$, then there exists a finite 
$$
\Sigma^0\subseteq\Sigma\mid \Sigma^0\vdash A
$$
> Theorem: Transitivity of deducibility
> Let $\Sigma\subseteq Form(L^p), A_1,\cdots A_n\in Form(L^p), n\leq 1$. 
$$
\Sigma\vdash A_i \text{ for all } i\in[1,n] \text{ and } A_1,\ldots A_n\vdash A\implies \Sigma\vdash A
$$
> Theorem: Reductio ad absurdum
$$
\Sigma, A\vdash B \text{ and } \Sigma, A\vdash\neg B\implies \Sigma\vdash\neg A
$$
### Theorem of replaceability of Syntactically Equivalent Formulas $(\vdash)$
> Definition: Two formulas $A,B$ are **syntactically equivalent** if 
$$
A\vdash B\text{ and }B\vdash A
$$
> Theorem: Replaceability of syntactically equivalent formulas
> Let $B, C$ be syntactically equivalent. For any $A$, let $A'$ be constructed from $A$ by replacing some (not necessarily all) occurrences of $B$ by $C$. Then $A, \neg A$ are syntactically equivalent.
### Theorems of Soundness and Completeness of Formal Deduction
> Theorem: Soundness Theorem (should not be able to prove incorrect statements)
$$
\Sigma\vdash A\implies \Sigma\models A
$$
> Theorem: Completeness Theorem (should be able to prove every correct statement)
$$
\Sigma\models A\implies \Sigma\vdash A
$$

**Connection between syntax and semantics**
The Soundness and Completeness Theorems say that with formal deduction (as defined by the 11 rules) we can prove
The truth,
the whole truth, (completeness),
and nothing but the truth. (soundness)

**Formal deduction proof strategies**
1. If the consequence is an implication is an implication ($\Sigma\vdash A\implies B$), use $(\implies, +)$
2. If one of the premises is a disjunction ($\Sigma, A\lor B\vdash C$), use proof by cases $(\lor, -)$
3. If we have to prove $C\vdash D$ and the direct proof doesn't work, proof the contrapositive $(\neg D\vdash\neg C)$
4. If all else fails, use proof by contradiction $(\neg, -)$ or $(\neg, +)$ in the case of $\Sigma\vdash\neg B$
### Consistency and Satisfiability
> Definition: Consistency
> A set of formulas $\Sigma$ is **consistent** (w.r.t. a system of formal deduction, herein $\vdash$) if there is no formula $F$ such that $\Sigma\vdash F$ and $\Sigma\vdash\neg F$. Otherwise, $\Sigma$ is **inconsistent**.

> Lemma: Satisfiability
> A set $\Sigma$ of logic formulas is satisfiable iff $\Sigma$ is consistent

## 1.7 | Automated Theorem Proving by Resolution
### A formal deduction system with a single rule: resolution
Resolution theorem proving is a method of formal deduction
- "a giga proof by induction"
- The only formulas allowed are disjunctions (OR)
- There is only one rule: resolution
$$
(C\lor p), (D\lor\neg p)\vdash (C\lor D)
$$
- To prove that $A_1,\ldots A_n\models C$ is valid, show that the following is **not satisfiable**
$$
\{A_1,\ldots, A_n, \neg C\}
$$
- Prove that from this set, we can derive both $B$ and $\neg B$
- Do this by converting everything to CNF
- The resolvent of $p, \neg p$ is called the empty clause, signifying a contradiction
$$
p, \neg p\vdash \{\}
$$
Example: Proving
$$
p, p\implies q\vdash q
$$
Proof:
1. $p$ (premise)
2. $\neg p\lor q$ (premise)
3. $\neg q$ (negation of conclusion)
4. $q$ (resolving 1, 2 over $p$)
5. $\{\}$ (resolving 3,4 over $q$)

### What is an algorithm?
A finite sequence of well-defined, computer-implementable instructions, typically to solve a class of problems or perform a computation.
### The Halting Problem
Halting Problem: Does there exist an algorithm (program) that operates as follows:
- Input: A program P , and an input I to the program.
- Output: “Yes” if the program P halts on input I, and “No” otherwise.

Proved unsolvable by Alan Turing
- Construct $K(P)$ which calls $H(P,P)$ and outputs the opposite of it's result
- Then you cannot tell if $K(K)$ halts
### Turing Machines as a formal model of computation
A Turing Machine is a 7-tuple $M=(Q,\Sigma,\Gamma,\delta,q_0, B, F)$ where
- $Q$ = the finite set of **states** of the finite control
- $\Sigma$ = the finite set of **input symbols**
- $\Gamma$ = the set of **tape symbols** such that $\Sigma\subset T$
- $\delta$ = the (partial) **transition function** such that $\delta:Q\times\Gamma\times\{L,R\}$, where L, R are "left" and "right"
- $q_0$ = the **start state**, a member of $Q$
- $B$ = the **blank** symbol, which is in $\Gamma$ but not $\Sigma$
- $F$ = the set of final, or **accepting** states, a subset of $Q$

## 3.16b | Undecidability of Universal Validity for First-Order Logic
### Decidability and computability
A decision problem for which there exists a terminating algorithm that solves it is called **decidable (solvable)**, otherwise it is **undecidable (unsolvable)**
- Solvable means there exists a total TM that accepts those and only those problem instances that lead to a “yes” answer

A total function that can be computed by a total Turing machine is called computable, otherwise it is called uncomputable.
- Uncomputable function example: the **Busy Beaver function**
- Let $B(n)$ be the max number of 1s that a TM with $n$ states and alphabet $\{1,B\}$ may print on a blank tape before halting

The satisfiability (is any set satisfiable) and universal validity (is any formula valid) problems for FoL are **undecidable**
### Proving undecidability
Reduce it to a previously solved problem. Suppose you can reduce $P_1$ to $P_2$:
- If $P_1$ is decidable, we can't conclude anything
- If $P_1$ is undecidable, so is $P_2$
- If $P_2$ is decidable, then so is $P_1$
- If $P_2$ is undecidable, we can't conclude anything
### Examples of undecidable problems 
**decider** = a total TM that solves a decision problem

## 4.18 | Formal Verification
### Introduction: What and Why?
Techniques for showing program correctness
- Inspection
- Testing:
	- Black-box: tests independent of code
	- White-box: tests based on code
- Formal design verification

Formal program verification:
- Formally state the specification of a program with FoL
- Prove that the program satisfies the specification for all inputs
- **Although this is undecidable** (no terminating algorithm), there are some techniques

Framework for formal verification
1. Convert the English description of $R$ to logic ($\Phi_R$)
2. Write a program $P$ meant to realize $\Phi_R$ in some given programming environment
3. **Prove that the program P satisfies** $\Phi_R$

Imperative programming: Defines control flow as statements that change a program states / describes how a program operators (FORTRAN, PASCAL, OOP languages)
Declarative programming: Focuses on what the program should accomplish without telling it how to do it (SQL, functional programming, HTML)

We are verifying imperative (uses variables), sequential (no concurrency), and transformational (turns inputs to outputs) languages. 

We use the core features of C/C++ and Java
- ints and bools
- assignment
- sequence
- `if-then-else` conditionals
- `while` loops
### Pre- and Post-conditions
Hoare Triples:
$$
(|P|)C(|Q|)
$$
- $(|P|)$ = precondition
- $C$ = program or code
- $(|Q|)$ = postcondition

Specification is not behaviour
- Instead of $(|y\cdot y<x|)$, write $(y\cdot y<x)\land\forall z((z\cdot z<x)\implies z\leq y)$
### Partial and Total Correctness
> Definition: Partial Correctness
> A Hoare triple is satisfied under partial correctness, denoted
$$
\models_{par}(|P|)C(|Q|)
$$
> if and only if an input satisfying $P$ given to $C$ terminates and satisfies $Q$

Partial correctness can be **vacuously satisfied** if the program never terminates
```cpp
while true { x = 0; } // infinite loop
```

> Definition: Total Correctness
> Partial correctness + every valid input terminates

**If the input is altered ("consumed"), a program is satisfied under NEITHER partial nor total correctness**
### A Formal Proof System for Partial Correctness
Rewriting the pre and postconditions for correctness using FoL.
Domain: Programs, program states, and conditions
- `State(s)` - "s is a program state"
- `Condit(P)` - "P is a condition"
- `Code(C)` - "C is a program"
- `Satisfies (s,P)` - "State s satisfies condition P"
- `Termiantes (C,s)` - "Program C terminates when given state s"
- `result (C,s)` - **A FUNCTION** that gives the state resulting from executing C with state s if C terminates (undefined otherwise)

Expressing correctness with FoL (Hoare triple $(|P|)C(|Q|)$)
Partial correctness
$$
\forall S\forall P\forall Q[State(S)\land Condit(P)|\land Condit(Q)\implies
$$
$$
(Satisfies(s,P)\land Terminates(C,s)\implies Satisfies(result(C,s), Q))]
$$
Total correctness 
$$
(Satisfies(s,P)\implies Terminates(C,s)\land Satisfies(result(C,s), Q))]
$$
### Assignments
To prove partial correctness, use **sound inference rules**
To prove termination, use **ad-hoc reasoning** (even though program termination is undecidable)

A partial correctness proof is an annotated program with justifications

> Inference rule for assignment *(assignment)*
$$
\overline{(|Q[E/x]|)x=E;(|Q|)}
$$
- If the condition(s) / Hoare triples above the line are proved, then the Hoare triple under the line holds
- This means "$Q(x)$ will hold after substituting all values of $x$ for E"
- $Q[E/x]$ is read as "Q with E in place of x" (x is a free variable)

Example ($Q(x) = x\approx 7, E = y+1$)
$$
(|y+1\approx 7|)=y+1;(|x\approx 7|)
$$

We usually work **backwards** from the postcondition
$$
\begin{align}
&?? &&(|Q[E/x]) \\
&x=y; &&x=E; \\
&(|x>0|) &&(|Q|)
\end{align}
$$

Inference rules about implications **(NOT FRACTIONS, BUT UNDERLINES)**
> Precondition strengthening *(implied)*
$$
\frac{P\implies P'\quad(|P'|)C(|Q|)}{(|P|)C(|Q|)}
$$
> Postcondition weakening *(implied)*
$$
\frac{(|P|)C(|Q'|)\quad Q'\implies Q}{(|P|)C(|Q|)}
$$

> Inference rules for instruction *(composition)*
$$
\frac{(|P|)C_1(|Q|),\quad (|Q|)C_2(|R|)}{(|P|C_1:C_2(|R|))}
$$
- We find a **midcondition** Q for which we can prove the top (like transitivity)

Most proofs are constructed from the **bottom upwards**
### Conditionals
*(if-then-else)*
$$
\frac{P(|P\land B|C_1(|Q|))\quad (|P\land\neg B|)C_2(|Q|)}{(|P|)\text{ if }(B) C_1 \text{ else } C_2(|Q|))}
$$
*(if-then)*
$$
\frac{P(|P\land B|C(|Q|))\quad (|P\land\neg B|)\implies(|Q|)}{(|P|)\text{ if }(B)  C_2(|Q|))}
$$
Proof strategy
1. Annotate the program using appropriate inference rules
2. "back up" the proof: add an assertion/condition before each assignment statement
3. Prove any "implieds" (annocations from 1, assertions from 2)
### While-Loops and Total Correctness
*(partial-while)*
$$
\frac{(|I\land B|)C(|I|)}{(|I|)\text{ while }(B) C(|I\land \neg B)}
$$
- $I$ = loop invariant (true both before and after each execution of the body of the loop)
